01 — The sky

Benjamin
Banneker

1731 — 1806

Mathematician and astronomer. In 1791 he assisted Major Andrew Ellicott on the survey of the land set aside for the federal district. His calculations of the sun, moon, and planets were printed in almanacs, including the 1792 Pennsylvania, Delaware, Maryland, and Virginia almanac, issued in Baltimore by William Goddard and James Angell.

A star chart drawn for this page. Not a plate from his almanac.

02 — The path

Katherine
Johnson

1918 — 2020

NASA mathematician. She calculated the trajectory for Alan Shepard’s Freedom 7 flight in May 1961, the first crewed American spaceflight. In 1962, before John Glenn’s Friendship 7 orbit, she rechecked the electronic computer by hand. NASA’s Langley biography records her memory of his reply: “If she says they’re good, then I’m ready to go.” She later calculated the trajectory for Apollo 11 in 1969.

Orbital paths drawn for this page. Not a mission diagram.

03 — The estimate

David
Blackwell

1919 — 2010

Statistician and mathematician, born in Centralia, Illinois. He took every degree at the University of Illinois — B.A. 1938, M.A. 1939, Ph.D. 1941 — writing on Markov chains with Joseph L. Doob. In 1965 he became the first African American named to the National Academy of Sciences. The Rao–Blackwell theorem, which he published in 1947 after C. R. Rao’s 1945 result, sharpens an estimate by taking its conditional expectation given a sufficient statistic. The variance does not increase. He worked in probability, game theory, and Bayesian statistics, and in 1979 received the John von Neumann Theory Prize. The University of Illinois also notes his independent work on dynamic programming.

δ₁(T) = E[ δ₀(X) | T ]

The field here is a picture of that idea: scattered guesses pull toward a sufficient statistic, and a game tree opens beside them. It is not a plot of his data.

  • National Academy of Sciences, 1965
  • Rao–Blackwell, 1947
  • von Neumann Theory Prize, 1979

04 — The cartridge

Jerry
Lawson

1940 — 2011

Engineer. At Fairchild he led the video-game division that brought the Channel F console to market in 1976, the first home system sold around interchangeable cartridges. A game could leave the machine. A library could exist. The cartridge hardware began as a prototype at Alpex. Lawson’s group made it something a person could buy games for.

An abstract cartridge and a slot. Not the Channel F, and not its marks.
