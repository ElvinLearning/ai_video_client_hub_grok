A lineage

Four people. Then a film.

A moving image sits downstream of measurement: an almanac, an orbit, a sharper estimate, a cartridge you could swap. These are still lifes of their work, painted for this page. They are not portraits.

01 — The sky

Benjamin Banneker

1731 — 1806

Mathematician and astronomer. In 1791 he assisted Major Andrew Ellicott on the survey of the land set aside for the federal district. His calculations of the sun, moon, and planets were printed in almanacs, including the 1792 Pennsylvania, Delaware, Maryland, and Virginia almanac, issued in Baltimore by William Goddard and James Angell.

02 — The path

Katherine Johnson

1918 — 2020

NASA mathematician. She calculated the trajectory for Alan Shepard’s Freedom 7 flight in May 1961, the first crewed American spaceflight. In 1962, before John Glenn’s Friendship 7 orbit, she rechecked the electronic computer by hand. NASA’s Langley biography records her memory of his reply: “If she says they’re good, then I’m ready to go.” She later calculated the trajectory for Apollo 11 in 1969.

03 — The estimate

David Blackwell

1919 — 2010

Statistician and mathematician, born in Centralia, Illinois. He took every degree at the University of Illinois — B.A. 1938, M.A. 1939, Ph.D. 1941. In 1965 he became the first African American named to the National Academy of Sciences. The Rao–Blackwell theorem, published in 1947, sharpens an estimate by taking its conditional expectation given a sufficient statistic. The variance does not increase.

04 — The cartridge

Jerry Lawson

1940 — 2011

Engineer. At Fairchild he led the video-game division that brought the Channel F console to market in 1976, the first home system sold around interchangeable cartridges. A game could leave the machine. A library could exist. The painting shows an abstract cartridge and slot, not the Channel F and not its marks.
